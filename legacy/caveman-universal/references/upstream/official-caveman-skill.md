# Caveman — cut the fluff

Small word good, if word carry meaning. Big empty phrase always bad, no matter how dressed up.

That's the spirit, not the execution. This skill does not produce broken grammar or clipped, unnatural sentences. It produces normal, professional prose that happens to contain zero words that aren't doing work.

## The test

For every sentence or clause, ask: **if I delete this, does the reader lose a fact, a number, a name, a decision, or a logical link?**

- No loss → cut it, or fold what's left into the sentence next to it.
- Real loss → keep it, exactly as precise as it was.

This test matters more than any list of banned phrases below. A phrase can be on the "cut" list and still be worth keeping in a specific sentence, if it's doing real work there. Judge the sentence, not just the words in it.

## Scope

Apply this when creating or editing **documents**: reports, essays, theses/papers, memos, articles, professional emails, proposals, summaries — anything that will be read as a finished written artifact.

Don't apply it to:
- Ordinary conversation, explanations, or back-and-forth chat — a natural, warmer register is usually what's wanted there, and clipping it can come across as cold or unhelpful.
- Emotional or supportive conversation.
- Creative writing (fiction, poetry, lyrics) — voice and rhythm are the point, not information density.

If it's ambiguous whether something counts as a "document," lean toward normal register. This skill should sharpen writing, not flatten every reply into a memo.

## What to cut

**Throat-clearing openers** — sentences that announce content instead of giving it.
> "It is important to note that the warehouse experienced delays." → "The warehouse experienced delays."

**Stacked hedges** — piling multiple qualifiers where reality only needs one (or zero).
> "It could possibly perhaps be the case that demand might increase." → "Demand may increase."

**Empty transitions** — connectors that don't connect any actual logic.
> "Moving forward, and with that being said, the team will..." → "The team will..."

**Restated conclusions** — saying a finding, then saying it again in summary form.
> "In conclusion, to summarize everything discussed above, delays were mainly caused by handover gaps." → "Delays were mainly caused by handover gaps."

**Nominalizations** — verbs smothered into noun phrases.
> "conduct an analysis of" → "analyze" · "make a decision on" → "decide" · "provide assistance to" → "help"

**Vague qualifiers standing in for a known fact** — say the number or name if it's known.
> "a significant number of shipments" → "62% of shipments" (if that's the actual figure — never invent one that isn't in the source)

**Redundant pairs** — two words doing one word's job.
> "each and every," "full and complete," "past history," "end result," "completely eliminate" → pick one word.

**Padding passive voice** — passive that hides a known, relevant actor and adds words to do it.
> "It was determined by the team that X caused the delay." → "The team found X caused the delay."
> Passive is fine, even correct, when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant, or when it's the field's convention — don't force active voice where it breaks how a discipline normally writes.

**Writing-about-the-writing** — sentences about the document instead of its content.
> "As mentioned previously," / "This section will now discuss..." → cut, unless it's a real navigational aid in a long document (a "(see §3.2)" cross-reference earns its place; a paragraph announcing what the next paragraph will say does not).

**Verbal-tic intensifiers** — "very," "really," "quite," "basically," "essentially," "actually" used as reflex rather than for meaning. Cut them, or if the emphasis is real, upgrade to one stronger word ("very important" → "critical").

## What never to cut

- **Facts** — numbers, names, dates, citations, specific findings. These are the entire point of a document. Never trade them for brevity, and never invent a specific figure or name to fill a gap the source left vague.
- **Genuine hedges in technical, medical, legal, or financial claims.** "May cause," "is associated with," "in this sample" often carry real information about certainty and scope. Tightening the sentence around a hedge is good; deleting the hedge itself can turn a true, careful claim into a false, overconfident one. When in doubt, keep the hedge.
- **Real logical connectors** — "because," "although," "therefore," "however" tell the reader how two ideas relate. That's information, not filler.
- **Required structure** — headings, citation formats, methodology sections, and other elements a genre or institution requires, even if they feel formulaic.
- **The author's voice**, when editing someone else's draft. Cut padding; don't sand off their phrasing, tone, or personality while doing it. A cover letter's warmth and a legal memo's neutrality are both intentional, not fluff.

## Editing an existing document

1. Work through it in natural chunks — paragraph by paragraph, or section by section for something long.
2. Apply the deletion test to each sentence.
3. If asked to produce a trimmed version: rewrite in place, keeping the original structure, terminology, and claims. Don't introduce new content or soften/strengthen a claim while cutting words around it.
4. If asked to review without a full rewrite: flag candidates rather than silently cutting — e.g. "Cuttable: '...' — restates the sentence before it" — so the person can approve changes to their own document.
5. Where it's useful, note the compression (e.g. "142 words → 89 words") so the person can see the effect.

## Drafting new text

1. Write the claim or fact first; don't lead with a sentence that just announces the claim is coming.
2. Draft, then reread once specifically hunting for throat-clearers, hedge-stacks, and nominalizations before presenting the result — the second pass is where most of this gets caught.
3. Default to active voice and concrete nouns, unless a hedge or passive construction is carrying real information (see above).

## Worked example

**Input** (61 words):
> It is important to note that, over the course of the observation period, a significant number of the shipments that were processed experienced delays which could possibly be attributed to a variety of different factors, including but not limited to handover procedures between shifts. In conclusion, it can be seen that handover procedures were found to be a major contributing factor.

**Output** (16 words):
> Many shipments processed during the observation period were delayed, mainly due to gaps in shift-handover procedures.

Same facts, same claim, no invented numbers — just no sentence left that only existed to introduce or repeat another one.
